The rule behind the comfort check

Every step asks your legs to lift by one riser and reach forward by one tread. In 1675 the French architect François Blondel noticed that a stair feels natural when twice the riser plus the tread matches an ordinary walking stride, about 630 millimetres. The rule has outlived almost everything else from his era; UK building regulations still use it, accepting anything from 550 to 700 mm.

Try it. Lower the tread and the stair gets steeper and shorter. Add risers and each one gets lower. Push either too far and the verdict changes. The figures here are a feel for the trade-off, not a design: real stairs also have to meet the local building rules, clear the headroom above them and fit the structure.

Why stairs eat floor space

A straight flight between floors of an ordinary home runs four to five metres, before any landing. That is why so many Indian houses turn the stair back on itself as a dog-leg, halving the length at the cost of a landing. The figure below the results shows the trade for your settings.
